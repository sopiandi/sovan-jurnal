# November 28, 2026 — What Happens When You Scan a Barcode

A barcode is a small picture that a machine can read faster than a person can type. Those black stripes are not random decoration: each pattern of wide and narrow bars stands for a number, and the scanner's light reads the pattern and turns it back into that number in a fraction of a second.

The number itself is the key. It usually identifies the product — which item it is, not which individual box — and the shop's system uses it to look up the name and the price on file. That is why the same product always scans to the same price, and why changing a price means updating the record once, not relabelling every item on the shelf.

At the counter, this does three quiet jobs. It removes typing mistakes, it speeds up the queue, and it keeps the stock count honest, because every scan is also a record that one item has left the shelf.

It is a good example of simple technology doing heavy work: a printed pattern, a beam of light, and a lookup table, turning a long queue into a short one.
